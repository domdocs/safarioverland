## 1. Positioning by channel

### Instagram — the view from the driver's seat

What it's for: making the drive itself desirable, and making it look doable. The reader should finish a scroll thinking *I could do that, and I'd want to.*

- **Who it's for:** US and UK travellers, roughly 35–65, who've done one guided safari or none, and are drawn to the idea of their own car without camping.
- **What it shows:** the road (gravel at four in the afternoon, a border post, a farm stall), the stays (a hide, a fire, a guide's hands on a track), and the competence behind them (a page of the road book, the vehicle handover).
- **What it avoids:** rooftop tents, recovery boards, "overlanding" kit culture, drone shots of trucks. That's the audience we're steering away from.
- **Voice:** *we*. Short. One sensory line, two sentences of context, link in bio.

### LinkedIn — Niels, the editor's argument

What it's for: arguing the case. Why lodge-based self-drive, why these routes, how a route gets drawn, what partners on the ground actually do. LinkedIn is where the honest second opinion lives at length.

- **Who it's for:** three audiences, in this order. (1) Professionals who travel independently and read long posts: future travellers. (2) The trade: lodges, DMCs, US and UK travel advisers who might refer self-drive clients they don't want to plan themselves. (3) Travel journalists.
- **What it shows:** the thinking behind the product, specific and occasionally argumentative. One idea per post.
- **Voice:** Niels's *I*, with *we* for Safari Overland. Signs off "— Niels". Links go in the first comment.

> A road book is the least glamorous thing we make, and the one I'd least want to travel without.
