It is the question we are asked most often, and the honest answer is that both are good first trips, for different people. Namibia is the country most people picture when they imagine a self-drive safari: empty gravel roads, red dunes, a desert coast, Etosha's white pan with elephants coming in to drink. Zimbabwe is the one fewer people have thought of, with shorter distances, greener country, the Falls at one end and some of the best guiding on the continent along the way.

We plan and book both, and our first partners on the ground are in Victoria Falls, so take our bias into account. What follows is the comparison as we see it, with the parts that suit some travellers and not others left in.

## Namibia: space, and a lot of gravel

Namibia is the most established self-drive country in the region, and it shows. Lodges are used to travellers arriving in their own vehicles, roads are signposted, and a classic loop from Windhoek through the Kalahari, Sossusvlei, the coast at Swakopmund, Damaraland and Etosha runs to two weeks or more without retracing your steps.

**What suits a first trip:**

- **Variety.** Desert, coast, mountains and one of Africa's great game parks in a single loop. Few countries give you that range from the driver's seat.
- **Self-drive is normal here.** Lodges, fuel stations and park staff are used to independent travellers, and the information you need is easy to find.
- **Etosha suits self-drivers.** Its waterholes and open roads are made for watching from your own car, at your own pace.
- **Low traffic.** On many days you will see few other vehicles.

**What to weigh:**

- **Gravel, and distance.** Most of Namibia's road network is gravel, and the distances are long. You need to be comfortable driving carefully on loose surfaces for hours at a time, well below the speed the empty road invites. Rollovers on gravel are the accident Namibian self-drive is known for.
- **Visas.** Since April 2025, UK and US passport holders need a visa, available on arrival or online.
- **Fuel in the parks.** Fuel at the government camps in Etosha and at Sesriem has been unreliable through 2025 and 2026, so days need planning around the towns.
- **Desert means desert.** Big skies, few people, and long empty stretches. Some travellers love it; some find a fortnight of it more than they wanted.

## Zimbabwe: shorter days, deeper guiding

Our Zimbabwe route runs from Victoria Falls to Hwange and on to the Matobo Hills near Bulawayo, ten to twelve nights. It is a smaller, greener trip than Namibia, and in some ways an easier drive.

**What suits a first trip:**

- **Mostly tar between the hubs.** The main roads linking Victoria Falls, Hwange and Bulawayo are tarred. The gravel and sand are inside the parks, where you can drive them yourself or leave them to a guide.
- **Shorter driving days.** Hwange's Main Camp is roughly 200 km from Victoria Falls, nearly all of it on tar. You spend more of the trip at the lodges and less of it at the wheel.
- **The guiding.** Zimbabwe has a long tradition of guiding on foot, and on this route it is close at hand: walking safaris in Hwange, rhino tracking in the Matobo Hills. This is a trip where the guided activities are at least half the point.
- **Victoria Falls at the start or end,** and the easiest link to Chobe and the four corners if you want to add a few nights.

**What to weigh:**

- **Cash and fuel.** Zimbabwe runs on US dollars, physical notes are short, and fuel is sometimes unavailable. It needs a little more preparation than Namibia: small notes, a full tank whenever you can, a plan B.
- **Roadblocks and potholes.** Police checkpoints are frequent, and the main roads are potholed in places. Neither is a problem if you drive slowly and carry your papers, but both take some getting used to.
- **Fewer self-drivers.** Lodges are less used to independent arrivals than in Namibia, and roadside information is thinner. That is part of the appeal, and part of why the road book and our partners on the ground matter more here.
- **Visas.** UK and US passport holders need a visa, available on arrival or online, paid in US dollars.

## Where Botswana fits

Botswana has some of the finest wildlife country in Africa, but for self-drivers its parks are camping country: deep sand, remote public campsites, a fully equipped 4×4 and the experience to use it. That is a different trip from the one we plan.

So we use Botswana at the edges. Chobe, from Kasane, is an easy addition to Victoria Falls, by road and on the river. The Makgadikgadi pans can be reached by road on a longer route. And the Okavango Delta works best the way most people visit it, by light aircraft from Maun: you hand over the keys for a few nights and let someone else do the driving. For a first self-drive we would not build a trip around Botswana. As a set piece within one, it is hard to beat.

## Which first?

**Choose Namibia if** you want landscape as much as wildlife, you are confident on long drives and happy on gravel, and you have two weeks or more. It is the classic first self-drive for good reason.

**Choose Zimbabwe if** you want the animals and the guiding to lead, you would rather drive shorter days, mostly on tar, and you are happy with a country that asks a little more preparation in exchange for fewer other vehicles. It also fits in ten nights.

**Join them, or add the four corners, if** you have the time. Victoria Falls is the hinge. A Zimbabwe route can return through Chobe and the Zambezi Region; a long Namibian route can run north and east to finish at the Falls.

## What we'd do

For most first-time self-drivers coming from the US or UK with ten or eleven nights, we would start with Zimbabwe: Victoria Falls, Hwange, Matobo, with a night or two at Chobe if time allows. The driving is gentler, the guiding is exceptional, and the hub is where our partners on the ground are.

For a traveller with two weeks or more, a love of open country and a steady hand on gravel, Namibia. Allow more time than you think, keep the driving days to what the road book says, and do not try to fit the whole country into one trip.
