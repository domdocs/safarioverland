Three small things decide whether a long driving day feels easy or anxious: how much fuel is in the tank, whether you have the right money in your pocket, and whether the phone knows where you are. None of them is difficult. All of them work differently here from the way they work at home, and the differences are worth knowing before you land.

## Fuel: fill up at half

The rule we drive by is simple. When the gauge reaches half and you pass a working fuel station, stop and fill up. Distances between reliable stations in Namibia can be long, a station marked on the map may be out of fuel or closed for the day, and a detour to find another can add 100 km or more.

Some specifics worth knowing:

- **Namibia's park camps.** Through 2025 and 2026, fuel at the government-run camps inside the parks, including those in Etosha and at Sesriem, has been unreliable or unavailable. Plan as if there is none. Fill up in the gateway towns instead: Outjo, Kamanjab, Tsumeb or Grootfontein for Etosha; Mariental or Maltahöhe for Sesriem and Sossusvlei, with Solitaire as a backup that has had limited supply. For the Skeleton Coast, fill at Henties Bay or Khorixas.
- **Zimbabwe.** Fuel is sometimes unavailable, with queues at the stations that do have it. The UK government advises topping up whenever possible. Inside Hwange, fuel at the camps has a long record of being unreliable, so arrive with a full tank and leave enough to get back out to the main road.
- **Know your fuel.** Check whether the car takes petrol or diesel at the handover, and say it clearly at the pump.

Fuel stations are also where things go missing. The UK government notes that theft from vehicles at Namibian service stations is common. Lock the car, keep valuables out of sight, and do not leave it unattended while you go inside.

## Paying in Zimbabwe: US dollars, small notes

Zimbabwe runs on US dollars. A local currency, Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG), was introduced in April 2024 and is in circulation, but the US dollar remains legal tender and is what most visitors use. In practice:

- **Bring cash, in small notes.** Physical US dollar notes are in short supply and change is often unavailable, so tens, fives and ones are worth far more to you than hundreds.
- **Fuel is usually paid in US dollars.** A 2026 survey of Harare fuel stations found most selling only in US dollars. Some stations take cards, many outside the main centres do not. Carry enough cash for fuel even if you expect to use a card.
- **Cards work at lodges and in Victoria Falls,** but not everywhere, and machines go down. Do not rely on them alone.
- **Know the rules on cash.** Declare US$10,000 or more on arrival. Leaving Zimbabwe with more than US$2,000 in cash is illegal. Change money only at banks or licensed bureaux de change.

Visa fees at the border are also paid in US dollars, which is another reason to arrive with a stack of small notes.

## Paying in Namibia, Botswana and Zambia

- **Namibia** uses the Namibian dollar, pegged one-to-one to the South African rand. Both are accepted. Cards are widely used at lodges, supermarkets and most fuel stations, but accepting cards is at each fuel retailer's discretion, so carry some cash for remote stations and for when the machine is down.
- **Botswana** uses the pula. Carry some for border charges and small purchases, alongside a card.
- **Zambia** uses the kwacha. Some border charges, including the carbon emission surcharge on vehicles, have to be paid in kwacha.

Tell your bank where you are going before you travel, carry two cards from different accounts, and keep them in different places.

## Mobile signal

Signal is better than most people expect in towns and along main roads, and absent where you would expect it to be absent: deep in the parks, along remote coastline, in the empty country between settlements.

- **Namibia.** The main network has the widest coverage, including along many of the long roads. Etosha's main camps have had a basic data signal. Expect none on much of the Skeleton Coast, in the far north-west and in the deep Namib.
- **Zimbabwe.** Expect signal in Victoria Falls and Bulawayo, gaps along the main roads between them, and little inside Hwange beyond the camps.
- **Botswana.** Expect signal in the towns and at main gates and lodges, and little or none deep inside the parks.

Lodges often have Wi-Fi, though it may be slow and only in the main area.

## eSIMs and local SIMs

An eSIM bought before you fly is the simplest option, but check two things. First, which network it actually uses in each country, since coverage varies by network. Second, whether it covers every country on your route: a plan for one country will not work across the border, and a trip from Victoria Falls through Kasane into the Zambezi Region touches three. Regional plans covering several southern African countries exist; read the list of countries and partner networks before buying.

A local SIM is cheap and usually gives the best coverage in that country, but expect to show your passport to register it, and you need one per country.

## Offline maps

Do not rely on live mapping. Before you leave home, download offline maps for each country on your route in the mapping app you use, and check that they cover the whole area, parks included. A paper map is still worth having.

The road book is designed to work without signal: it is on paper, and the phone version works offline, with each day's route, distances, road surfaces, fuel stops and gate times.

## What we'd do

Fill up at half, every time, and never plan around a park camp's pump. Fly into Zimbabwe with a few hundred US dollars in small notes, more if you are driving far, and a card as backup. Buy a regional eSIM that lists each country on the route and accept that the parks are offline. Download the maps before you leave home.

Then put the phone away for the drive. The line is there for when it matters; the rest of the time, the road is the point.
