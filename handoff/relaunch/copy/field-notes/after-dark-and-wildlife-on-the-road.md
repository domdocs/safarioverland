The last hour of the day is the best hour to be on the road here. The light goes long and amber, the dust hangs gold behind the car, and the animals start to move: kudu stepping out of the mopane, a line of elephants heading for the river, cattle being walked home along the verge. It is also the hour to be arriving, not still driving. Once the sun is down, everything that makes these roads beautiful becomes something you cannot see until it is in your headlights.

This note covers one rule we do not bend, and what to do when the road surprises you anyway.

## Why we don't drive after sunset

The UK government's advice is the same for Zimbabwe and Namibia: avoid driving outside towns at night. The reasons are specific.

- **Animals.** Wildlife and livestock are on and beside the roads, and many are dark-coloured and still. A cow standing on tar at night is very hard to see until you are close to it. A kudu or an elephant can step out of the bush with no warning at all.
- **Unlit vehicles and people.** In Zimbabwe, the UK government lists unlit vehicles, including heavy trucks and cyclists, and pedestrians among the main road hazards.
- **Potholes.** On Zimbabwe's main roads, deep potholes that you would steer round in daylight are hard to see at night, and hitting one at speed can burst a tyre or worse.
- **Help is further away.** If something does go wrong after dark, emergency services in Zimbabwe may offer very limited help, and in remote Namibia the nearest anyone may be a long way off.

Some rental contracts also restrict cover for night driving. Check yours.

## Plan to arrive by mid-afternoon

The way to avoid driving in the dark is to plan the day so you never need to. The road book gives each day a realistic drive time for each section, with the surface named, and an arrival time well before sunset. Leave after breakfast, allow for a fuel stop, a slow stretch, a border queue and an elephant, and aim to reach the lodge by mid-afternoon. Then the last golden hour is spent on a guided drive or at a hide, not watching the clock.

If a day starts to run late, because of a puncture or a long border, call ahead to the lodge and call the line. It is better to shorten the day than to finish it in the dark.

## Park gates follow the sun

National park gates in this part of the world open around sunrise and close around sunset, and you are expected to be either out of the park or inside your camp by closing time.

- **Etosha.** Gate times are set by sunrise and sunset and change through the year, often week by week. They are posted at the gates and at the camps. You must be out of the park or inside a camp by sunset; arriving late can bring a fine.
- **Hwange.** Gate and camp times follow the light too. Times vary between camps and areas of the park, so take them from the road book or the camp reception rather than from memory.

Wherever you are, aim to be at your gate or camp at least half an hour before closing. Distances inside parks are slow: a 40 km drive back to camp on sand roads, with game stopping you on the way, can easily take two hours.

## Elephants in the road

Sooner or later on our routes, an elephant will be standing in the road. Usually it is a pleasure. Occasionally it is not. What to do:

- **Stop well back and wait.** Give it space: the more the better. Let it cross or move off in its own time. Elephants have right of way.
- **Stay in the car.** Do not get out for a photograph, however calm it looks.
- **Keep the engine running and quiet.** Do not rev it, hoot, or try to push past or nudge the animal off the road.
- **Never drive between members of a herd,** or between a mother and calf.
- **Back off if it is unsettled.** Ears spread, head high, a trunk swinging, a step or two towards you: reverse slowly to give it more room, and keep reversing if it follows.

Other large animals on the road get the same courtesy: stop, wait, let them go.

## Livestock and smaller animals

Outside the parks, cattle, goats and donkeys are a common sight on the roads, in Zimbabwe especially and around villages and farms anywhere on our routes. They move unpredictably, and where there is one there are often more. Slow down as soon as you see animals near the road.

If something runs out in front of you, brake firmly in a straight line. Do not swerve. On gravel especially, a swerve is far more likely to roll the car than the impact is to hurt you.

## If you break down

- **Get off the road if you can.** Pull well over, put the hazard lights on, and set out the warning triangles.
- **In a park, stay in the vehicle.** Do not walk for help in country with lions and elephants. Wait for another car or a ranger patrol.
- **Call for help.** Call the rental company's emergency number and our line. Tell us where you are: the day's page in the road book gives the route and distances, and if your phone can find a location, send it.
- **Stay with the car.** Outside parks, a vehicle is easier to find than a person, and it is shade.
- **Drink water.** Carry several litres in the car at all times, more in Namibia.

Our partners on the ground can arrange a recovery, a replacement vehicle or a lift to the next lodge. The hardest part is usually the wait, which is why the road book asks you to set off early: a breakdown at ten in the morning is an inconvenience, at five in the afternoon it can mean a night in the car.

## What we'd do

Leave after breakfast and plan to arrive by mid-afternoon, every day. Treat the park gate time as fixed. Slow down for anything with legs near the road, and let the elephants win.

Keep five litres of water, the warning triangles, a torch and a charged phone in the car. And if a day is slipping, call the lodge and the line early, rather than racing the light.
