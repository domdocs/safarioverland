The first ten minutes are the strangest. You walk to the wrong side of the car. You reach for the gear lever with the wrong hand and find the door. You indicate a turn and the wipers sweep across a dry windscreen. Then the road out of Victoria Falls or Windhoek opens up, the traffic thins to a truck and a donkey cart, and by the first fuel stop the left side has started to feel like a side, not a mistake.

Zimbabwe, Namibia, Botswana and Zambia all drive on the left. So does South Africa. If you are coming from the United States or Canada, this is the single biggest adjustment of a self-drive trip here, and it is smaller than it sounds. This note covers the practical part: which side, which gearbox, which paperwork, and what to do when a roundabout appears.

## The left side, and what actually changes

You sit on the right of the car and drive on the left of the road. The centre line sits by your right shoulder. That is the reference most drivers find easiest to hold on to: keep yourself, the driver, towards the middle of the road.

What changes in practice:

- **Turning right crosses traffic.** A right turn is the long, exposed one, the equivalent of an American left. A left turn is the short, tucked-in one.
- **Overtaking happens on the right.** On a two-lane road you pull out to the right to pass, which means you need a long, clear view of oncoming traffic from a seat that is on the wrong side for seeing round the truck ahead. Hang back, give yourself room, and pass only on a long straight.
- **The controls are mirrored, the pedals are not.** Accelerator on the right, brake in the middle, clutch (if there is one) on the left, as at home. The indicator stalk is often on the right of the steering column and the wipers on the left. You will mix them up. Everyone does.
- **Speeds and distances are in kilometres.** 100 km/h is about 62 mph; 80 km/h is about 50. Signs, rental contracts and the road book all use kilometres.

The moments to watch are the ones where your instincts take over: pulling out of a fuel station onto an empty road, leaving a lodge car park early in the morning, turning into a road with no traffic to follow. With no other car to copy, the old habit pulls you to the right. A small note on the dashboard (keep left) helps more than it should.

## Manual or automatic

Most rental fleets in southern Africa are built around manual gearboxes. In Namibia, automatics are less common and there is usually less choice of vehicle. They exist, including in the 4×4s most lodge-based travellers need, but they go first in busy months.

If you drive a manual at home every day, the gear lever under your left hand takes an hour or two to settle. If you don't, this is not the trip to learn. Changing gear with the wrong hand while reading an unfamiliar road on the wrong side is too many new things at once, and gravel punishes a missed gear on a hill.

Our advice to most American drivers is simple: ask for an automatic, and ask early. We match the vehicle to the route when we book it, with an automatic where one is available, and a gearbox you don't have to think about is part of that match.

## The International Driving Permit

An International Driving Permit (IDP) is a translation of your licence into a standard format. It does not replace your licence; you carry both. It is cheap, it lasts a year, and for this part of the world it is worth having even where it is not strictly required.

**From the United States.** The State Department has designated AAA to issue IDPs, and AAA's permit is valid for a year from its effective date. You can apply through a local AAA office or by post. The State Department and the Federal Trade Commission have both warned about websites selling unofficial "international licences" for large sums. They are not IDPs. Buy only from an authorised issuer.

**From the United Kingdom.** The Post Office stopped selling IDPs in March 2024. They are now sold at PayPoint shops, at £5.50 at the time of writing. There are three types, and the one you need depends on the country. The UK government lists a 1949 permit for Namibia and for Botswana, and notes that car hire companies there need one. It lists a 1968 permit for Zimbabwe. A trip that joins Zimbabwe to Botswana or Namibia may therefore need two permits. Check the government's country list before you go, since it changes.

Whatever your passport, carry your home licence and the IDP together, in the car, along with copies of your passport and visa pages. Rental desks ask for them. So do police at roadblocks.

## Roundabouts

Roundabouts are common at town entrances in Zimbabwe, Namibia and Botswana, and they are where American drivers most often hesitate.

On the left side of the road, traffic goes round clockwise. Give way to vehicles already on the roundabout, which means looking right as you approach. Enter when there is a gap, keep left, and indicate left as you pass the exit before yours. On roundabouts with more than one lane, follow the lane markings. If there are none, or you are unsure, stay in the left lane and go round slowly. Nobody minds.

Two other things you will meet in towns. Traffic lights are often called robots, which is useful to know when someone gives you directions. And power cuts can switch the lights off in Zimbabwe, so a junction you expected to be controlled may not be. Treat a dark set of lights as a junction where everyone gives way, and go carefully.

## Roadblocks and the rest

Police roadblocks and checkpoints are a normal part of driving here, especially in Zimbabwe. Slow down, stop when signalled, be polite, and have your licence, IDP and the vehicle's papers to hand. If a fine is due, ask for a receipt. The UK government also advises that if the presidential motorcade approaches in Zimbabwe, you pull off the road and stop. Keeping going is illegal.

At the fuel station, know whether the car takes petrol or diesel before you pull in. It is on the handover sheet; write it on the dashboard note too. How you pay is a note of its own.

## What we'd do

Ask for an automatic where one is available, even if it costs a little more, and book it with the lodges rather than afterwards. Get the IDP before you fly: AAA if you are American, the right type or types from a PayPoint shop if you are British.

Keep the first day short. A handover at Victoria Falls and a drive of an hour or two to the first lodge is ideal; a long day on gravel straight off a long-haul flight is not. Our partners on the ground do the handover and will sit with you for the first few minutes if you want them to: mirrors, lights, which stalk does what. The road book sets out that first drive, surface by surface, so you know what is coming before you turn the key.

And if something feels wrong on the road, pull over somewhere safe and call the line. It is what it is for.
