Search for a self-drive safari and the pictures are nearly all the same: a white double-cab pickup with a tent folded on the roof, a fridge in the back, jerry cans on a rack, a campfire at dusk. It is a fine way to travel. It is also a lot of vehicle, a lot of kit and a lot of evenings spent pitching, cooking and packing up, when you could be sitting on a deck with a drink while someone who knows the area tells you what came to the waterhole last night.

If you are sleeping in camps and lodges, the vehicle question changes. You do not need a house on wheels. You need a car that suits the roads between the places you are staying, and a contract that does not leave you exposed when a stone meets the windscreen. This note sets out how we think about it.

## Three kinds of vehicle

**SUV (two-wheel drive or all-wheel drive).** A higher-riding car than a saloon, with more ground clearance. Comfortable on tar and good gravel, easy to drive, cheaper to hire and to fuel. It is not built for deep sand or rough tracks, and some rental contracts restrict where you may take it.

**4×4.** A true four-wheel-drive vehicle with low-range gearing, usually a double-cab pickup or a large SUV built on the same chassis. Higher clearance, tougher tyres, better on corrugations, sand and the rough tracks inside some parks. Heavier and thirstier, and on some models more work to drive in town.

**Camper.** A 4×4 fitted out for camping: one or two rooftop tents, a fridge, a water tank, cooking kit, chairs, sometimes a second battery. Excellent if you are camping. If you are not, you are paying to carry equipment you will never unpack.

## Why lodges let you skip the rooftop tent

Lodge-based travel strips the vehicle back to what the road needs. No tent, no fridge, no camping kit, no evenings spent on logistics. That brings three practical gains.

- **A lighter, steadier car.** A loaded roof puts weight high up, which is the wrong place on loose gravel and on cambered bends. An unloaded 4×4 or SUV is easier to control, and easier to recover from a slide.
- **Less to go wrong.** No awnings to secure, no fridge draining the battery, no stove to light in the wind.
- **More of the day for the place itself.** At a lodge you arrive, someone takes your bags, and the afternoon is yours: a guided drive, a walk, a hide. Guided activities mean you are not limited to what you can see from your own driver's seat.

## Which roads need which car

The vehicle follows the route, not the other way round.

- **Zimbabwe, Victoria Falls to Hwange and Matobo.** The main roads between the hubs are tar, often potholed. Inside Hwange, the game-viewing roads are gravel and, in places, deep Kalahari sand. A 4×4 lets you use them; in an SUV you rely more on the lodge's guided drives, which is no hardship.
- **Namibia.** Most of the road network is gravel, and distances are long. A high-clearance vehicle is the sensible minimum, and a 4×4 is our default for the full loop. At Sossusvlei, a tar road runs from the park gate to a car park about 5 km short of the pans, and the last stretch is soft sand. Self-drive access on that stretch has been restricted and reopened more than once in 2025 and 2026; a shuttle runs from the car park either way. The road book gives the rule as it stands when you travel.
- **Botswana and the four corners.** Kasane and the tarred road through the Zambezi Region are manageable in an SUV. The parks themselves are deep-sand camping country, which is one reason we use Botswana at the edges of our routes rather than at the centre.

## Insurance: read the exclusions

This is the part people skim, and the part that matters most. Rental cover in southern Africa usually comes in tiers, with a lower excess the more you pay. What separates a good policy from a poor one is what it leaves out. Ask, in writing, how your cover treats:

- **Tyres and windscreens.** Commonly excluded, or covered only by an extra option. On gravel, both are the most likely damage you will have.
- **Underbody and water damage.** Rocks, sand ridges and river crossings. Often excluded.
- **Single-vehicle accidents.** If you roll the car with nobody else involved, are you covered, and for how much? The UK government warns that many hire policies in Namibia do not cover accidents that do not involve another vehicle or an animal.
- **Night driving and unapproved roads.** Some contracts restrict cover if you drive after dark or on roads the company excludes.
- **Cross-border use.** Cover should extend to each country on your route, and the cross-border letter should match.

Take the highest tier of cover the company offers, with tyre and glass cover added. Separate excess insurance from a third party can help, but check its own exclusions, which often mirror the ones above.

## Two spare tyres

For Namibia, and for any route with long stretches of gravel, ask for two spare tyres. The UK government advises it for Namibia. Some vehicles come with two; others come with one, and a second can usually be added. A single puncture is routine. A second, on a long gravel road with no spare left, is a day lost.

At the handover, check both spares, the jack and the wheel spanner, and that the jack fits that vehicle. Practise once.

## Manual or automatic

Manual gearboxes are more common in the fleets here, and automatics are fewer and book out first. If you do not drive a manual every day at home, ask for an automatic early. It is one less thing to think about on an unfamiliar road.

## What we'd do

For a lodge-based route in Namibia: a 4×4 without camping kit, automatic if you want it, two spare tyres, and the top tier of cover with tyres and glass included. For Zimbabwe: a 4×4 if you want to drive Hwange's sand roads yourself, a good SUV if you would rather leave that to the guides. For the four corners, an SUV is usually enough if you stay off the park tracks.

We book the vehicle with the lodges, matched to the roads in the road book, with the cross-border letter arranged. Our partners on the ground hand it over, walk round it with you, and are the people you call if it lets you down.
