Gravel is where the drive becomes the safari. The tar ends, the car takes on a low drumming note, and a plume of pale dust unrolls behind you for a kilometre. A kudu stands in the road and considers you. Nothing else moves for an hour. It is also where most self-drive trips in southern Africa go wrong, and nearly always for the same reason: speed carried from the tar onto a surface that does not hold a car the same way.

In Namibia you cannot avoid it, and you would not want to. Of a national road network of about 49,000 km, roughly 8,000 km is tarred; the rest is gravel, earth and salt. In Zimbabwe the main routes between Victoria Falls, Hwange and Bulawayo are tar, and the gravel begins inside the parks: the game-viewing loops in Hwange, the granite country of the Matobo Hills. This note is about driving both well.

## Speed: slower than the sign

Whatever the sign allows, the number that matters on gravel is the one at which you can still steer, brake and react to something you did not expect. The UK government's advice for Namibia is not to exceed 80 km/h on gravel. We treat 80 as a ceiling for a good, wide, freshly graded road with clear sight lines. On narrow roads, on corrugations, near dips and on bends, 50 to 60 is often right.

It feels slow. Namibian distances are long, and the temptation after an hour of empty, straight road is to let the needle drift. Resist it. A gravel road can change surface within a few metres: hard-packed to loose, smooth to sand, flat to a dip with a sandy riverbed at the bottom. At 100 km/h you meet that change before you see it.

## Why cars roll

The rollover is the classic self-drive accident in Namibia. The pattern is familiar to anyone who works with rental vehicles there:

1. The car is travelling too fast for loose gravel.
2. Something prompts a sudden movement: a bend that tightens, a patch of deep gravel, an animal, an oncoming car in a cloud of dust.
3. The driver brakes hard or swerves. The wheels lose grip on the loose surface, the back of the car steps out, and the driver over-corrects. The car slides sideways, a tyre digs into the soft verge, and it goes over.

The cure is mostly speed, and the rest is technique. Keep your hands steady and your inputs small. If the car starts to slide, ease off the accelerator and steer gently where you want to go; do not stamp on the brake. Brake in a straight line before a bend, not in it. Stay out of the ridge of loose gravel that builds up along the edges and down the middle of the road. And if an animal steps out, brake firmly in a straight line rather than swerving. Swerving is what rolls the car.

Wear seatbelts in the back as well as the front. Keep heavy luggage low and secured, not loose on the back seat.

## Tyres and pressure

Tyres are the part of the car that does the work on gravel, and the part most rental insurance excludes.

- **Pressure.** Use the pressures the rental company gives you for gravel, and check them in the morning before you drive, when the tyres are cold. Hot tyres read noticeably higher. Common advice among people who drive these roads is that slightly lower pressures than on tar give better grip and fewer stone cuts on rough gravel, but under-inflated tyres run hot at speed. Ask at the handover what they want you to run, and stick to it. Deep sand is a separate matter, with much lower pressures, and only if your vehicle and your briefing cover it.
- **Two spares.** For Namibia, carry two spare tyres. The UK government advises it, and anyone who has had a second puncture 150 km from the nearest tyre repair will tell you why. Check both at the handover, along with the jack and wheel spanner.
- **After a puncture.** Change the wheel, then get the damaged tyre repaired or replaced at the next town that can do it, before you go further. Driving on with no spare is how a puncture becomes a long wait.

Learn to change a wheel on your vehicle before you need to. Ten minutes in the car park at the handover, with someone watching, is worth more than any guide.

## Dust and other cars

On a dry road, an oncoming car brings a wall of dust that can hide everything behind it for several seconds, including a second car, a cyclist or a cow. Slow right down as it approaches, move to the left, and do not drive into dust you cannot see through. Keep your headlights on, day and night, so others see you sooner.

Following another vehicle, drop back far enough to stay out of its dust, and out of range of the stones its tyres throw. Overtake only when you can see a long, clear stretch, and expect the driver ahead not to see you.

Windscreens crack. It is common enough on gravel that many rental policies exclude glass, and it is one more reason not to sit close behind a truck.

## Rain, dips and riverbeds

Many Namibian gravel roads deteriorate in the rainy season, which the UK government gives as January to April. Dips and dry riverbeds that are dull in September can hold water or soft sand after a storm. Slow down for any dip signs, look before you drive into water, and if you cannot see the bottom or are unsure of the depth, wait or turn back. Ask at the lodge about the road ahead; they will know.

## Wildlife on gravel

The animals you came to see also use the roads. In Namibia, springbok, kudu, oryx and ostrich all cross without warning, as do cattle and goats near farms and villages. Inside Hwange the roads are game-viewing routes, and the elephant at the next bend is the point of being there. Drive as if something is about to step out, because sooner or later it will. That is another reason the speeds above are lower than you might expect.

## What we'd do

Plan the days so that the gravel does not have to be driven fast. That is half of what the road book is for: daily distances with the road surface named and an honest drive time for each section, so there is no need to make up time on loose stones in the last hour of light.

Take the second spare, a tyre pressure gauge you trust, and the full cover for tyres and glass if it is offered. Learn to change a wheel at the handover. Drive the first gravel day well under the limit, and notice how the car moves when you brake. Let the dust settle before you pass anyone.

And if the car is damaged, or simply feels wrong, stop somewhere safe and call the line. Our partners on the ground can arrange help faster than you can find it yourself.
